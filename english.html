English question bank (Edexcel 1EN0 + 1ET0)

Language and Literature: every question type, answer frames, themes to prepare and model full-mark answers.

In English the question types are fixed and appear on every paper (a certainty, not a guess); what changes is the extract and the theme. So the plan is to master each question type with a formula and prepare every major theme for each text.

English Language Paper 1 — Fiction and imaginative writing (64 marks)

Q Marks Appears Model answer frame
1 1 Every paper One short quotation or paraphrase from the exact lines named. Stay inside the line range.
2 2 Every paper Two separate points, each with a brief quotation.
3 6 Every paper Three mini-paragraphs: The writer uses [technique] "[quote]" … the word "[x]" suggests … this makes the reader feel … Include at least one structural point (opening, shift in focus, sentence length).
4 15 Every paper Evaluate the statement: "I largely agree because…"; 4–5 paragraphs covering beginning, middle and end of the section; judge how successfully the writer creates the effect, using words like "vividly", "effectively", "powerfully".
5/6 40 (24 content + 16 SPaG) Every paper: choice of two prompts, one with images 5-minute plan of 5–6 paragraphs; vary openers (adverb, -ing verb, preposition); one deliberate one-line paragraph; range of punctuation (; : — ? !); a cyclical ending that echoes the opening image.

Most frequent imaginative prompts (estimated from the papers since 2017): a story about a time something went wrong or changed (very high), a description prompted by one of two images (very high), stories about journeys, mysteries, discoveries or being lost (high). Prepare 2 adaptable "core" stories that can bend to any of these.

English Language Paper 2 — Non-fiction and transactional writing (96 marks)

Q Marks Appears Model answer frame
1, 2, 4, 5 1–2 each Every paper Short and exact; do not overwrite.
3 15 Every paper Analyse language AND structure of Text 1 with a clear line on the writer's purpose and viewpoint.
6 15 Every paper Evaluate how successfully the writer of Text 2 achieves the stated effect; open with a judgement.
7a 6 Every paper Synthesis: 3 short points, each linking Text 1 and Text 2 with "Both… / Whereas…".
7b 14 Every paper Compare ideas AND methods across the whole of both texts; every paragraph mentions both; use "In contrast", "Similarly".
8/9 40 (24 + 16 SPaG) Every paper: choice of two Correct form features; audience named in the opening; DAFOREST devices used sparingly; counter-argument then rebuttal; call to action.

Transactional forms seen most often (estimate): article (very high), letter (high), speech or talk (medium), review/report/guide (lower). Practise one of each per month; use the school's writing tasks booklet for prompts.

English Literature Paper 1 — Macbeth and An Inspector Calls (80 marks)

Section Question Marks Model answer frame
A Macbeth (a) "Explore how Shakespeare presents [X] in this extract" 20 (AO2) Language, form (verse/prose, soliloquy) and structure in the extract only; 4 paragraphs; zoom on single words; dramatic effect on the audience.
A Macbeth (b) "Explain the importance of [X] elsewhere in the play" 20 (AO1 + AO3) 3–4 moments from across the play (beginning, middle, end); Jacobean context woven in (James I, divine right, witchcraft).
B An Inspector Calls One essay from a choice of two 32 + 8 SPaG (AO1, AO3, AO4) Thesis about Priestley's purpose; track the character/theme through Acts 1–3; 1912 vs 1945 context; the Inspector as Priestley's mouthpiece; careful SPaG.

Themes and characters to prepare (rotates every year, so prepare all):

  • Macbeth: ambition, guilt, power and kingship, the supernatural, violence, loyalty and betrayal, Lady Macbeth, Macbeth's changing character, appearance vs reality. PMT Macbeth notes · School knowledge organiser
  • An Inspector Calls: responsibility, class, gender, age/generations, guilt; Mr and Mrs Birling, Sheila, Eric, Gerald, the Inspector, Eva Smith. PMT notes · School booklet

English Literature Paper 2 — Jekyll and Hyde and poetry (80 marks)

Section Question Marks Model answer frame
A Jekyll and Hyde (a) "Explore how Stevenson presents [X] in this extract" 20 (AO2) Language and structure in the extract; Gothic setting, imagery of darkness, animal imagery.
A Jekyll and Hyde (b) "Explain the importance of [X] elsewhere in the novel" 20 (AO1 + AO3) 3–4 moments; Victorian context (respectability, science vs religion, Darwin, duality).

Model answers for each question type

These are original examples written to the standard of the top level in the Edexcel mark schemes. Real past questions, mark schemes and examiner exemplars are on the PMT and Pearson links above; use these models to learn the shape of a full-mark answer, then write your own.

Language Paper 1, Q3 (6 marks) — practice extract

The fog came down upon the harbour like a shroud. Somewhere a bell tolled, slow and patient, as if counting the drowned. Martha pulled her shawl tighter. She did not run. She would not run.

Question: How does the writer use language and structure to create tension?

Full-mark answer: The writer creates a threatening atmosphere through the simile "like a shroud", which links the fog to death and burial, suggesting that something fatal is about to happen. The verb "came down" makes the fog seem deliberate, almost predatory. The personified bell, "slow and patient", is unsettling because patience implies it is waiting; the clause "as if counting the drowned" makes the reader fear Martha may be next. Structurally, the writer shifts from the wide view of the harbour to a close focus on Martha, narrowing our attention to her vulnerability. The final short sentences, "She did not run. She would not run.", slow the pace; the repetition with the modal verb "would" reveals her determination, leaving the reader anxious about what she is choosing to face.

Why it scores 6: three precise points, technique named, single words analysed, effect on the reader, and one structural point.

Language Paper 1, Q4 (15 marks) — how to open

"I largely agree that the writer makes the ending tense. The writer builds tension skilfully by…; however, the tension is perhaps less effective at… because…"

Then 4–5 paragraphs moving through the extract in order, each judging how successfully the effect is created.

Language Paper 1, Section B (40 marks) — imaginative writing

Task: Write about a time when you were lost.

5-minute plan: 1 Present moment, sensory description of the station at night → 2 Flashback: arriving, the confidence → 3 The missed stop, panic rising (short sentences) → 4 One-line paragraph: the turning point → 5 A stranger's help → 6 Cyclical ending returning to the station clock.

Model opening: Midnight. The station clock hung above me like a pale, unblinking moon, its hands frozen in disapproval. Around me, the platform stretched into nothing: no trains, no voices, only the hum of a broken vending machine and the hiss of rain on the tracks. I had been confident once. Three hours, two wrong turnings and one dead phone ago, I had been very confident indeed.

Why it works: one-word opening sentence, simile and personification, triple structure, humour and a hint of backstory, varied sentence lengths and accurate punctuation.

Language Paper 2, Q7b (14 marks) — comparison paragraph frame

Both writers present [idea], but in different ways. Writer A [method] when they describe "[quote]", which suggests… In contrast, Writer B [method], using "[quote]" to… This difference reflects their purposes: Writer A wants to… whereas Writer B aims to…

Language Paper 2, Section B (40 marks) — transactional writing

Task: Write an article for a newspaper arguing that school uniform should be abolished.

Plan: headline + strapline → hook (anecdote) → point 1 cost (statistic) → point 2 self-expression (expert view) → counter-argument (equality) and rebuttal → call to action.

Model opening: Blazers, Ties and Broken Budgets: Time to Rethink the Uniform. Every September, parents across Britain perform the same anxious ritual: queuing, measuring, paying. For what? A polyester blazer that will be outgrown by Easter.

Literature Paper 1, Macbeth (a) (20 marks) — model paragraph

Task: Explore how Shakespeare presents Lady Macbeth as powerful in this extract (Act 1 Scene 5).

Model paragraph: Shakespeare presents Lady Macbeth's power through her commanding language when she calls on the spirits to "unsex me here". The imperative verb shows she believes she can order supernatural forces, and the verb "unsex" reveals her wish to reject the gentleness expected of women so that nothing can weaken her ambition. Her later plea, "Come, thick night", uses darkness imagery to hide her intentions, suggesting that her power depends on concealment. In soliloquy form, the audience sees her true thoughts, which makes her more threatening than any character on stage realises.

Literature Paper 1, Macbeth (b) (20 marks) — context woven in

Model sentence pattern: "Elsewhere in the play, Lady Macbeth's power collapses; in the sleepwalking scene her broken prose contrasts with the confident verse of Act 1. A Jacobean audience, who believed that killing a king defied God's order (the divine right of kings), would see her guilt as divine punishment."

Literature Paper 1, An Inspector Calls (32 + 8 marks) — essay opening

Task: How does Priestley present the theme of responsibility?

Thesis: Priestley uses the Inspector as a mouthpiece to argue that society must take collective responsibility for its most vulnerable members, contrasting the older Birlings' selfish capitalism with the younger generation's capacity to change.

Paragraph pattern: Point about Priestley's purpose → short quotation (e.g., the Inspector's warning that "we are members of one body") → analysis of a key word ("body" suggests society is one organism) → context (written 1945, set 1912; Priestley's socialism and the post-war welfare state) → effect on the audience.

Literature Paper 2, Jekyll and Hyde (a) (20 marks) — model paragraph

Task: Explore how Stevenson presents Hyde as violent in this extract (the murder of Carew).

Model paragraph: Stevenson presents Hyde's violence as animalistic through the phrase "ape-like fury", which links him to Darwin's ideas about humans' primitive ancestry and suggests that violence is the natural state beneath civilised behaviour. The contrast with Carew, described as an elderly and polite gentleman, intensifies the horror: Hyde destroys the very respectability Victorian society valued. The witness's distant viewpoint creates mystery, because the reader, like the maid, sees the attack but cannot understand it.

Literature Paper 2, poetry comparison (20 marks) — frame

Both poets explore [theme], but whereas [Poet A] presents it as…, [Poet B] suggests… [Poem A] uses [method] in "[quote]", which… Similarly/In contrast, [Poem B]… The poets' contexts help explain this: …

Write three such paragraphs (ideas, language, form/structure) and a one-line conclusion naming the deepest difference.

Unseen poetry (20 marks) — 30-minute routine

  1. Read both poems twice; write a one-line summary of each poem's message.
  2. Annotate three methods per poem (imagery, form, tone shift).
  3. Write three comparative paragraphs; quote briefly from both poems in each.
