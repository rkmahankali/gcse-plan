Edexcel changed the History question formats from summer 2025 and trimmed some content from summer 2026, so Aikya must practise the new formats; papers from 2018–2024 are still excellent for content but some question numbers and marks differ (Pearson summary of the changes). Model answers for each question type

Paper 1 — Crime and Punishment and Whitechapel

Q1a (2). Describe one feature of housing in Whitechapel in the 1880s.

Many families lived in overcrowded "rookeries" or lodging houses (feature), where a single room might house a whole family and lodgers paid by the night (supporting detail).

Q2b (4). How could you follow up Source A (a police report on a robbery) to find out more about policing problems in Whitechapel?

Detail I would follow up: the report says officers could not find the suspect in the narrow alleys. Question I would ask: how far did Whitechapel's street layout hinder H Division's investigations? Type of source: Charles Booth's poverty maps of the area. How this might help: they show the density of courts and alleys and which streets were considered most dangerous.

Q3 (4). Explain one way in which prisons in the 18th century were different from prisons in the late 20th century.

In the 18th century, prisons mainly held people awaiting trial or execution or debtors, and prisoners often had to pay gaolers for food and bedding, with men, women and children mixed together. In the late 20th century, prison itself was the punishment, and the focus included rehabilitation, with education and training provided and open prisons for low-risk offenders.

Q4 (12). Explain why the Bloody Code was abolished in the 19th century. (Stimulus: juries; transportation.)

Juries: By 1810 over 200 crimes carried the death penalty, but juries increasingly refused to convict for minor theft because they felt hanging was disproportionate. This meant the law was not being enforced, so it no longer deterred crime, which encouraged reformers to reduce the number of capital crimes.

Transportation: Transportation to Australia (from 1787) offered an alternative punishment that removed criminals without killing them, so judges and governments had a practical replacement for execution.

Reformers and new ideas (own knowledge): Enlightenment thinkers argued punishment should fit the crime and reform the offender. As Home Secretary in the 1820s, Robert Peel removed the death penalty for over 100 crimes, putting these ideas into law. Together these factors meant hanging was reserved mainly for murder and treason by the 1860s.

Q5 (16 + 4 SPaG). "The main reason for changes in policing c1700–c1900 was the growth of towns." How far do you agree?

Frame: Paragraph 1 agrees: rapid urbanisation in the industrial revolution increased crime and made older methods (parish constables, watchmen) inadequate, leading to the Bow Street Runners (1749) and the Metropolitan Police (1829). Paragraph 2 agrees further: the 1856 Act made forces compulsory as towns everywhere grew. Paragraph 3 disagrees: government attitudes and individuals mattered: Peel's political will created the Met; fear of protest and riots after events such as Peterloo pushed governments to want an organised police force. Conclusion: urbanisation created the need, but government action and individuals such as Peel turned that need into change, so it was the main underlying reason but not sufficient alone.

Paper 2 — Cold War (period study)

Q1a (4). Explain one consequence of the Berlin Blockade (1948–49).

One consequence was the formation of NATO in April 1949 (consequence). The blockade convinced Western powers that the USSR was a military threat to Western Europe, so the USA and Western European states agreed that an attack on one would be an attack on all (detail), which formally divided Europe into rival military blocs.

Q2 (8). Write a narrative account analysing the key events of the Cuban Missile Crisis, 1962.

After the failed US-backed Bay of Pigs invasion (April 1961), Castro moved closer to the USSR, and Khrushchev agreed to place nuclear missiles on Cuba. This led to crisis when an American U-2 spy plane photographed missile sites in October 1962. As a result, Kennedy announced a naval "quarantine" (blockade) on 22 October rather than an air strike, buying time for negotiation. Tension peaked on 27 October when a U-2 was shot down, but secret talks followed: Khrushchev agreed to remove the missiles in return for a US promise not to invade Cuba and the secret removal of US missiles from Turkey. The crisis ended on 28 October and led directly to the hotline (1963) and the Limited Test Ban Treaty (1963).

Q3 (16, 8 per part). Explain the importance of the Marshall Plan for relations between the USA and USSR.

The Marshall Plan (1947) offered around $13 billion of aid to rebuild Europe. It was important because Stalin saw it as "dollar imperialism", an attempt to spread US influence and capitalism, and he forbade Eastern Bloc countries from accepting it. This deepened the division of Europe. It also led the USSR to set up Cominform (1947) and Comecon (1949) as rival organisations, institutionalising the split between East and West.

Paper 2 — Henry VIII (British depth study)

Q2 (12). Explain why Wolsey fell from power in 1529. (Stimulus: the annulment; the Amicable Grant.)

Annulment: Henry wanted Wolsey to secure an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon so he could marry Anne Boleyn. When the Blackfriars court with Cardinal Campeggio was adjourned in 1529 without a verdict, Wolsey had failed in Henry's most important wish, destroying the King's trust.

Amicable Grant (1525): Wolsey's attempt to raise money for war without Parliament provoked resistance and had to be abandoned; Henry was humiliated and Wolsey's reputation for competence was damaged.

Enemies at court (own knowledge): The Boleyn faction and nobles such as the Duke of Norfolk resented Wolsey's power and used his failure to turn Henry against him; he was charged with praemunire and later arrested for treason, dying in 1530.

Q3 or 4 (16). "The main reason for the dissolution of the monasteries was money." How far do you agree?

Frame: Agree: the Valor Ecclesiasticus (1535) showed great monastic wealth; smaller monasteries were closed in 1536 and larger ones by 1540, and the Crown gained land and income. Disagree: Cromwell and reformers argued monasteries were corrupt (visitation reports); dissolving them removed loyalty to the Pope after the break with Rome; selling land to nobles bound them to the Reformation. Conclusion: money was the most significant reason because the scale of the Crown's gain explains why the larger houses were also closed, but religion and the need for loyalty made the policy possible.

Paper 3 — Weimar and Nazi Germany

Q1 (4). Give two things you can infer from Source A (a 1923 photo of a woman burning banknotes in a stove) about hyperinflation.

Inference 1: money had become almost worthless. Detail: she is burning notes for heat because they are cheaper than fuel. Inference 2: ordinary families were suffering hardship. Detail: she is using money just to keep warm at home.

Q2 (12). Explain why hyperinflation happened in Germany in 1923. (Stimulus: reparations; the Ruhr.)

Reparations: The Treaty of Versailles required reparations of £6.6 billion; when Germany failed to pay in 1922, it triggered a crisis.

Occupation of the Ruhr: In January 1923 France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr to take goods instead. Workers went on passive resistance and the government paid them, while losing the Ruhr's industrial output and tax income.

Printing money (own knowledge): To pay strikers and debts the government printed vast amounts of money, so prices rose out of control; by November 1923 a dollar was worth over 4 trillion marks. Each factor fed the next: reparations led to the occupation, which led to the printing.

Q3d (16 + 4 SPaG). "Interpretation 2 says Hitler became Chancellor mainly because of the Depression." How far do you agree?

Frame: Paragraph 1 supports Interpretation 2 with own knowledge (unemployment around 6 million by 1932; Nazi vote rose to 37% in July 1932). Paragraph 2 uses Interpretation 1 (if it stresses political scheming) with the "backstairs intrigue" of von Papen and Hindenburg in January 1933. Paragraph 3 adds Nazi propaganda and the SA. Conclusion: the Depression created mass support, but Hitler became Chancellor because elites chose him, so Interpretation 2 is only partly convincing.
