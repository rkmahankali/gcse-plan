Science question bank (AQA 8461 / 8462 / 8463)

AQA Biology, Chemistry and Physics: frequent questions per topic, required practicals and 35 worked answers.

AQA science papers return to the same question styles every year: a required practical, a calculation, a "describe/explain" chain and a 6-mark extended answer appear in every topic cluster. The rows below list the questions seen most often since the 9–1 papers began in 2018.

Likelihood is an estimate of how often each question style appears in a paper series, based on the pattern of past papers, not an official statistic: Very high = most years; High = about two years in three; Medium = about half. Every topic can still be examined.

For every topic: open the PMT topic page (revision notes, flashcards, and topic questions with mark schemes), do the questions, mark against the scheme, log lost marks. Full papers: AQA past papers and mark schemes · PMT Biology, Chemistry, Physics papers.

6-mark answer method (all sciences): plan 6 linked points in the margin; use the command word (describe = what happens in order; explain = why, using "because/so/therefore"; evaluate = for, against, judgement); use key terms exactly; no repeated points.

Worked questions with full-mark answers

Original questions in the style that recurs on AQA papers, with answers written the way the mark scheme credits them (each bullet is roughly one mark). Real questions and mark schemes: links above.

Biology

B1 Magnification (2). An image of a cell is 25 mm long; the real cell is 0.05 mm. Calculate the magnification.

  • Magnification = image ÷ actual = 25 ÷ 0.05 = ×500

B2 Osmosis practical (2). A potato cylinder goes from 2.50 g to 2.20 g. Calculate the percentage change in mass.

  • (2.20 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −12%

B3 Zone of inhibition (2). A clear zone has diameter 12 mm. Calculate its area.

  • r = 6 mm; πr² = π × 36 = 113 mm²

B4 Enzymes (3). Explain why the rate of an enzyme reaction falls above 40°C.

  • High temperature changes the shape of the active site / enzyme denatures
  • The substrate no longer fits the active site
  • Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form, so the rate falls

B5 Vaccination (4). Explain how vaccination protects against measles.

  • A dead or inactive form of the pathogen is injected
  • White blood cells produce antibodies specific to its antigens
  • Memory cells remain in the blood
  • On infection, antibodies are produced rapidly and in large numbers, destroying the pathogen before symptoms develop

B6 Inverse square law (2). A lamp moves from 10 cm to 20 cm from a plant. What happens to light intensity?

  • Light intensity ∝ 1/distance²; doubling the distance makes it ¼ of the original

B7 Blood glucose (6-marker, describe). Describe how blood glucose is returned to normal after a meal.

  • Blood glucose concentration rises after digestion of carbohydrates
  • The pancreas detects the rise
  • The pancreas releases insulin into the blood
  • Insulin causes glucose to move from the blood into cells
  • In liver and muscle cells glucose is converted to glycogen for storage
  • Blood glucose falls back to normal (negative feedback)

B8 Genetic cross (3). Two carriers of cystic fibrosis (Ff × Ff) have a child. What is the probability the child has cystic fibrosis?

  • Punnett square gametes F, f × F, f → FF, Ff, Ff, ff
  • ff has the disorder → 1 in 4 (25%, 0.25)

B9 Natural selection (6-marker, explain). Explain how bacteria become resistant to antibiotics.

  • Bacteria show variation due to random mutation
  • A mutation makes some bacteria resistant to the antibiotic
  • When the antibiotic is used, non-resistant bacteria die
  • Resistant bacteria survive and reproduce
  • They pass on the resistance gene to offspring
  • Over many generations the resistant strain becomes more common

B10 Quadrats (3). Mean 4 daisies per 0.25 m² quadrat; field area 2000 m². Estimate the population.

  • 4 ÷ 0.25 = 16 per m²
  • 16 × 2000 = 32 000 daisies

Chemistry

C1 Reacting masses (3). Calculate the mass of CO₂ made when 25 g of CaCO₃ decomposes (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; Ar Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16).

  • Mr CaCO₃ = 100; moles = 25 ÷ 100 = 0.25
  • Ratio 1 : 1 → 0.25 mol CO₂
  • 0.25 × 44 = 11 g

C2 Titration (4). 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol/dm³ NaOH is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of HCl. Calculate the concentration of HCl.

  • Moles NaOH = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol
  • Ratio 1 : 1 → 0.00250 mol HCl
  • 0.00250 ÷ 0.0200 = 0.125 mol/dm³

C3 Atom economy and yield (3). CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Calculate the atom economy for CaO. If 11 g of CO₂ was expected but 8.8 g was collected, what is the percentage yield?

  • Atom economy = 56 ÷ 100 × 100 = 56%
  • Yield = 8.8 ÷ 11 × 100 = 80%

C4 Bond energies (3). H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (H–H 436, Cl–Cl 242, H–Cl 431 kJ/mol). Calculate the energy change.

  • Bonds broken = 436 + 242 = 678 kJ
  • Bonds made = 2 × 431 = 862 kJ
  • 678 − 862 = −184 kJ/mol (exothermic)

C5 Structure and bonding (4). Explain why sodium chloride has a high melting point.

  • Giant ionic lattice
  • Strong electrostatic forces of attraction between oppositely charged ions
  • In all directions
  • Large amounts of energy are needed to overcome them

C6 Graphite (3). Explain why graphite conducts electricity and is soft.

  • Each carbon forms three covalent bonds, leaving one delocalised electron per atom that moves through the structure and carries charge
  • Layers are held together by weak forces
  • So layers can slide over each other

C7 Electrolysis of brine (4). Name the products and write the half equation at each electrode.

  • Cathode: hydrogen; 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂
  • Anode: chlorine; 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
  • Sodium hydroxide solution remains

C8 Rates and collision theory (3). Explain why increasing temperature increases the rate of reaction.

  • Particles have more energy and move faster
  • More frequent collisions
  • More collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, so more frequent successful collisions

C9 Equilibrium (3). N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. Explain the effect of increasing pressure on the yield of ammonia.

  • The position of equilibrium shifts to the side with fewer gas molecules
  • 4 molecules on the left, 2 on the right
  • So the equilibrium shifts right and the yield of ammonia increases

C10 Chromatography (2). A spot moves 3.0 cm; the solvent front 7.5 cm. Calculate Rf.

  • 3.0 ÷ 7.5 = 0.40

C11 Ion tests (recall set). Flame: Li⁺ crimson, Na⁺ yellow, K⁺ lilac, Ca²⁺ orange-red, Cu²⁺ green. NaOH: Cu²⁺ blue, Fe²⁺ green, Fe³⁺ brown precipitate; Al³⁺ white, dissolves in excess. Acidified AgNO₃: Cl⁻ white, Br⁻ cream, I⁻ yellow. Acidified BaCl₂: SO₄²⁻ white. Gases: H₂ squeaky pop, O₂ relights glowing splint, CO₂ limewater milky, Cl₂ bleaches damp litmus.

Physics

P1 Kinetic energy (2). A 1200 kg car moves at 15 m/s.

  • Eₖ = ½ × 1200 × 15² = 135 000 J

P2 Specific heat capacity (2). Energy to heat 2.0 kg of water by 10°C (c = 4200 J/kg°C).

  • ΔE = mcΔθ = 2.0 × 4200 × 10 = 84 000 J

P3 Efficiency and power (3). A motor uses 500 J and gives 400 J useful energy in 20 s.

  • Efficiency = 400 ÷ 500 = 0.8 (80%)
  • Useful power = 400 ÷ 20 = 20 W

P4 Circuits (3). 4 Ω and 6 Ω in series with a 12 V supply. Find the current and the energy transferred in 2 minutes.

  • Total R = 10 Ω; I = V/R = 1.2 A
  • Q = It = 1.2 × 120 = 144 C; E = QV = 144 × 12 = 1728 J

P5 Density (2). Mass 54 g, volume 20 cm³.

  • ρ = m/V = 2.7 g/cm³

P6 Latent heat (2). Energy to melt 0.50 kg of ice (L = 334 000 J/kg).

  • E = mL = 167 000 J

P7 Half-life (2). Count rate falls from 800 to 200 in 12 days.

  • 800 → 400 → 200 is two half-lives → half-life = 6 days

P8 Decay equation (2). Uranium-238 (92 protons) emits an alpha particle.

  • ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He (mass number down 4, atomic number down 2)

P9 Motion (3). A car accelerates from rest at 2 m/s² over 100 m.

  • v² − u² = 2as → v² = 2 × 2 × 100 = 400
  • v = 20 m/s

P10 Momentum (3). A 2 kg trolley at 3 m/s hits a stationary 1 kg trolley; they move together.

  • Momentum before = 2 × 3 = 6 kg m/s
  • Momentum is conserved: 6 = 3 × v
  • v = 2 m/s

P11 Waves (2). f = 50 Hz, λ = 0.20 m.

  • v = fλ = 10 m/s

P12 Motor effect (2). B = 0.20 T, I = 3.0 A, l = 0.50 m.

  • F = BIl = 0.30 N

P13 Transformer (2). 230 V primary; 1000 primary turns, 50 secondary turns.

  • Vs = 230 × 50/1000 = 11.5 V

P14 Required practical, 6-marker. Describe how to find the specific heat capacity of an aluminium block.

  • Measure the mass of the block on a balance
  • Insert heater and thermometer; wrap the block in insulation
  • Record the starting temperature; connect heater to joulemeter (or ammeter, voltmeter and timer)
  • Switch on; record temperature every minute for 10 minutes and the energy supplied
  • Plot temperature against energy; gradient = 1 ÷ (mc)
  • Calculate c = ΔE ÷ (mΔθ); insulation reduces energy loss to the surroundings
